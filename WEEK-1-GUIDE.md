# Integrated Personal Growth - Skills Training Week 1

**Topic:** Interrupting automatic defensiveness; learning STOP, Missing-Links Analysis, and Fruzzetti's *How to Stop Making Things Worse*  
**Status:** Proposed initial Week 1 training package; not evidence that reading or practices have been completed.  
**Learning rhythm:** Tuesday = one-hour training; Wednesday through Sunday = actual-life practice; following Monday = one-hour review.  
**Meditation:** 10 minutes daily of breath-anchored mindfulness with gentle noting (thinking, sensing, etc.), then return to the anchor.

## 1. Objectives

By the end of the learning cycle, aim to be able to:

1. State what each letter of standard DBT STOP asks you to do.
2. Recognize an observable sign that a habitual defensive response is occurring, including *after* you have begun to explain.
3. Interrupt the *next* unhelpful sentence or escalation without equating a pause with surrender or agreement.
4. Rehearse one constructive response after STOP.
5. Use Missing-Links Analysis when an intended effective behavior does not occur.
6. Record learning, rehearsal, opportunity, actual response, and uncertainty as separate facts.

These are learning objectives, not a diagnosis, a professional treatment plan, or a requirement to tolerate hostility or abandon boundaries.

## 3. Skill A - STOP (standard DBT)

Linehan teaches this as a crisis-survival skill for not acting impulsively in ways that make a situation worse. Its four components are:

- **S - Stop:** Refrain from the immediate impulse. For this custom practice, the relevant moment may occur after a first defensive sentence, when you catch yourself starting a second.
- **T - Take a step back:** Briefly pause, disengage from the immediate push to explain, and allow room for a choice. A step back does not require leaving the conversation.
- **O - Observe:** Notice what is happening internally and externally. A simple observable cue is sufficient; you do not need to retrieve a named emotion.
- **P - Proceed mindfully:** Consider what would help or worsen the interaction, your goals, and relevant other people's needs. Select an action, which may be listening, acknowledgement, clarification, respectful disagreement, a boundary, or a break.

STOP does **not** decide who is correct, require agreeing with an accusation, or guarantee that the interaction improves. The response after STOP needs separate practice.

### Application: a caught-in-the-middle interruption

**Fictional rehearsal:** Someone says, "You never pay attention to what I ask." You begin to explain the exceptions, then notice you are repeating your explanation.

- Catch: "I am explaining again."
- Stop the *next* rebuttal, even though the first one already happened.
- Take a brief pause; remain present if appropriate.
- Observe the other person's actual concern and your impulse to correct.
- Choose one response matched to the context. Examples include asking what the main concern is, acknowledging a specific valid part, or saying that you want to understand the impact before discussing factual differences.

This is a custom application combining Linehan and later relational response skills, not a verbatim script from Linehan.

### Common mistakes to watch for

1. Treating the first defensive remark as proof that the whole skill was missed. The next remark is another possible intervention point.
2. Using STOP to silence yourself indefinitely or automatically concede an inaccurate claim.
3. Attempting a complete emotion search under activation when a simple behavioral cue is already apparent.
4. Using STOP as a substitute for learning how to listen, validate, disagree, or set a boundary.
5. Making every uncomfortable conversation into a crisis.

## 4. Skill B - Fruzzetti's Chapter 3

Chapter 3 focuses on *stopping additional harm* before trying to improve a relationship. Several actual source elements are especially relevant:

- **Commitment through advance rehearsal:** Practice an effective response while calm rather than relying on intention alone under stress.
- **Self-righteousness and retaliation:** Feeling entitled to react harshly because another person was harsh may make a conflict worse. This is not an instruction to accept hostile treatment.
- **Stepping back without surrender:** Choosing not to escalate can protect self-respect; it does not establish agreement.
- **Anticipating triggers and rehearsing an alternative:** Identify the type of statement or circumstance that predictably evokes the old response; mentally rehearse a different ending.
- **Ending a conflict constructively:** Prepare language for genuine concern, overwhelm, or a break when needed. Avoid scripted statements you do not actually mean.
- **Managing destructive urges:** Chapter 3 offers observing urges and imagining consequences of following or not following them.

**Personalization still untested:** It is plausible that repetition, a raised voice, an urge to correct a characterization, or faster speech could become reliable detection cues, based on prior self-reports. The first week's record should identify which cues were actually noticed rather than presuming a certain trigger occurred.

## 5. Skill C - Missing-Links Analysis (standard DBT)

Use this when an intended effective behavior *did not happen*, rather than for explaining a behavior that did happen.

Linehan's Handout 8 asks about four distinct potential obstacles:

1. **Knowledge:** Did I know what behavior was called for?
2. **Willingness:** If I knew, was I willing to do it?
3. **Recall/salience:** If willing, did the idea of doing it occur to me at the relevant time?
4. **Execution:** If it occurred to me, what prevented me from doing it right then?

Only investigate a step supported by the actual record. Do not assume avoidance or unwillingness when a practice simply did not come to mind.

Illustrative branches:

- Did not know how to use STOP -> study/rehearse the skill.
- Did not want to use it -> explore the competing goal or meaning (for example, a belief that stopping means surrendering), with curiosity rather than accusation.
- It did not come to mind -> use an environmental cue, phone reminder, or a repeatedly rehearsed trigger-response plan.
- It came to mind but was not performed -> clarify the precise barrier and practice a smaller executable step.

The same logic applies to forgetting a diary entry, mindfulness, or an intended caring action.

## 6. Training exercise - fictional scenarios

Choose a scenario and work with ChatGPT **one question at a time**. Do not invent a personal memory to fill an exercise.

### Scenario A - inaccurate interpretation

Someone says, "You only care about your own convenience." You believe the statement is unfair. You begin listing several exceptions.

1. At what point could you notice your own behavior (even if late)?
2. What is the *next* behavior you might interrupt?
3. Can you preserve the right to disagree while showing that you are trying to understand the concern?
4. What alternative sentence or action would you rehearse?

### Scenario B - accurate feedback

Someone points out a specific task you committed to but didn't complete. You notice yourself producing a detailed explanation of why it was difficult.

Ask whether explanation is relevant before deciding it is appropriate; practice acknowledging the observable miss and identifying the next action when justified.

### Scenario C - disrespectful or escalating interaction

Someone raises their voice, interrupts, and insists that you accept their interpretation of your intentions. Rehearse the difference among a non-retaliatory pause, respectful disagreement, a boundary, and an appropriate break. Do not assume continued engagement is mandatory.

## 7. Rehearsal protocol for the first practice week

**Three to five brief sessions** (2-4 minutes each):

1. Pick one fictional or previously discussed *manageable* scenario.
2. Imagine a triggering sentence for 10-15 seconds.
3. Rehearse recognizing one observable cue.
4. Mentally or aloud practice STOP.
5. Rehearse **one specific** constructive response; the same response does not need to fit every scenario.
6. Repeat with a slight variation. Record "rehearsed" but not "used in real life."

Do not manufacture a real conflict in order to test STOP. In actual life, use the practice only if a relevant opportunity occurs.

## 8. Daily practice (initial pilot)

- 10 minutes: breath-anchored mindfulness with gentle noting.
- 2-4 minutes: conflict rehearsal on scheduled days.
- In ordinary life: notice possible defensive responses, even mid-sentence; choose appropriate action.
- 3-5 minutes evening: record whether you completed practice and whether a relevant real-life opportunity occurred.

Suggested minimal record:

| Field | Valid response |
|---|---|
| Mindfulness | Done (minutes) / not done |
| Rehearsal | Done / not done / not planned |
| Real-life opportunity | Yes / no / unsure |
| First observable cue | Brief phrase / none noticed / unsure |
| Intervention | Before response / after starting / during escalation / afterwards only / not applicable |
| Next action | Brief observed behavior or "not sure" |
| Significant incident? | Short factual note and optional flag for deeper review |

Do not equate "no reported defensiveness" with a verified improvement in defensiveness. Distinguish no opportunity, no behavior noticed, uncertainty, and skipped records.

## 9. Tuesday training hour (flexible)

- 0-10 min: review objective, Linehan Handout 4 (STOP), and Handout 8 (Missing Links).
- 10-25 min: read/review Fruzzetti Chapter 3 (may be prepared ahead of the session).
- 25-40 min: Dr. Jennifer May STOP video, or selected portion if too long.
- 40-55 min: ChatGPT-guided scenario rehearsal; one question at a time.
- 55-60 min: identify cue-response practice for Wednesday-Sunday and note what to record.

If reading takes longer, prioritize comprehension and rehearsal over a rigid video quota. Completing the whole Fruzzetti chapter before Tuesday is an optional time-saving preparation.

## 10. Following Monday review

Bring the diary/export or brief practice notes. The weekly review should first establish what is known, what was attempted, and what remains untested.

- If an unwanted response occurred, select one relevant event for **behavior chain and solution analysis**.
- If an intended effective behavior did not occur (including rehearsing or using STOP), consider **Missing-Links Analysis**.
- If no relevant real-life conflict occurred, review practice adherence, simulated rehearsal, and other-oriented behavior without inventing a problem.
- Rehearse an adjusted alternative and then either retain Week 1's skill as the active target or select a new target. Completing a lesson does not automatically establish mastery.
